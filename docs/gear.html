The Studio

Built on Curiosity

People often ask me if I had a master plan when building this studio.

The answer is simple:

Absolutely not.

There was no spreadsheet, no five-year strategy, no dream shopping list pinned to the wall. This studio was built piece by piece, one late-night discovery at a time. I spent countless hours browsing second-hand websites, music forums, and classified ads, waiting for a machine to catch my eye and spark that familiar feeling of excitement.

Sometimes it was the sound.

Sometimes the design.

Sometimes simply the promise that it could teach me something new.

If a synthesizer made me curious enough to imagine the music we could create together, it found its way into the studio.

Over the years, those chance encounters grew into the collection you see today.

At the centre of everything sits the Akai Force, placed directly in front of me like the captain at the helm of a ship. Around it live the instruments I return to most often: the Moogs, the Dreadbox machines, the companions whose controls have become second nature to my hands.

The rest of the family waits patiently on a rack to my right.

A glance away.

Ready whenever inspiration decides to take an unexpected turn.

This studio isn't a museum built to impress.

It's a living workspace. A playground. A laboratory of happy accidents.

Every instrument here earned its place by making me feel something.

01

The Brain

Akai Force

The Conductor

Akai Force

At the centre of my setup sits the Akai Force: the conductor of this electronic orchestra.

It is far more than a sampler. It is my drum machine, MIDI sequencer, performance hub, and creative sketchbook. Every riff begins with human hands on real instruments. I play melodies and basslines live on each synthesizer, capturing the MIDI notes in the Force so they can be replayed while I continue shaping the sound directly from the hardware.

This allows me to focus on what I love most: opening filters, twisting knobs, adding movement and imperfections, and letting each machine reveal its personality in real time.

The Akai Force is also remarkably flexible. Through MIDI and USB, it connects and synchronises more than twenty synthesizers and drum machines, keeping the entire setup breathing together as one living instrument.

In many ways, it is the brain of the studio: remembering what has been played, coordinating every voice, and leaving my hands free to transform performance into something spontaneous and alive.

02

The Moogs

Soul, Muscle, and Madness

If the Akai Force is the brain of the setup, the Moogs are undoubtedly its soul.

The Charmer

Moog Subsequent 37

The Subsequent 37 has earned its reputation as one of the most iconic bass machines ever built, and deservedly so. Its low end is thick, warm, and impossible to ignore. But reducing it to a bass synthesizer misses half of its personality.

Beyond the growling basslines lies an extraordinary lead instrument. With its expressive modulation possibilities and evolving movements, it can sing, whisper, or scream. There is something unmistakably "Moog" about its sound. Even in the busiest mix, the Subsequent 37 somehow finds a way to make itself heard.

The Pocket Giant

Moog Minitaur

The Minitaur is often introduced as a dedicated bass synth. That's true — but only if you lack imagination.

Hidden behind its compact chassis is a remarkably expressive instrument. Its two oscillators, envelopes, and LFO allow it to produce far more than earth-shaking lows. It excels at simple yet unforgettable melodies that carry a surprising emotional weight.

Built like a tank and barely larger than a pair of hands, the Minitaur has become one of my most faithful companions. It travels with me to every live performance, proving that greatness doesn't always require a large footprint.

The Queen

Moog Matriarch

Part synthesizer, part instrument of chaos, part work of art.

A four-note paraphonic, semi-modular monster, the Matriarch refuses the comfort of certainty. There are no presets to save. Every patch exists only in the moment it is created. Every sound is temporary. Every performance becomes unique.

It demands your full attention and rewards experimentation with textures and colours unlike anything else in my studio. Its sound is vast, organic, and alive. Once the Matriarch enters a mix, it rarely settles into the background. It becomes part of the track's identity.

Many synthesizers strive for perfection. The Matriarch strives for character. And for that reason, it remains one of the most inspiring instruments I have ever played.

03

The Greek Gang

Dreadbox

If the Moogs are refined storytellers, the Dreadbox machines are wild poets.

Built in Greece and entirely analogue, these synthesizers are simpler than their American cousins. They offer less control, fewer safety nets, and little interest in behaving properly. Their oscillators drift. Their resonance screams. Their imperfections refuse to be corrected.

And that is precisely why I love them. They don't whisper, "Listen to me." They shout, "We exist!"

The Beast

Dreadbox Erebus V2

The Erebus V2 is simplicity itself: two oscillators, a handful of classic waveforms, a creamy analogue filter, and a wonderfully unstable analogue delay. On paper, it shouldn't be extraordinary.

In reality, it shakes walls.

Its bass can rattle the furniture, and when its filter begins to cry, even the neighbours become unwilling participants in the performance. There is something primal about the Erebus V2. It doesn't strive for sophistication. It aims directly for your instincts.

The Mad Scientist

Dreadbox Erebus V3

The Erebus V3 takes everything its older brother does and asks: what happens if we push things further?

A third oscillator enters the picture, capable of acting as an additional LFO. Ring modulation and cross-modulation open the doors to stranger territories, while frequency modulation introduces metallic textures and unexpected harmonics.

The V2 is raw power. The V3 is experimentation. It invites accidents, rewards curiosity, and constantly tempts you to turn one knob too far just to discover what lies beyond.

The Dream Painter

Dreadbox Typhon

The smallest member of the family, yet perhaps the most surprising.

Unlike the Erebus siblings, the Typhon isn't obsessed with brute force. Its two analogue oscillators remain wonderfully alive, but they surrender part of their control to simplicity. You don't sculpt every detail. Instead, you collaborate with the machine and let it guide you toward places you hadn't planned to visit.

And then come the effects. Its extraordinary digital effects engine transforms sounds into landscapes. Delays dissolve into mist, reverbs stretch beyond the horizon, and modulation paints movement and colour onto every note.

The Erebus machines scream. The Typhon dreams. Sometimes, listening to it feels less like hearing a synthesizer and more like standing in front of an Impressionist painting — where details disappear, emotions take over, and what matters most is not what you see, but what you feel.

04

The Dream Weavers

Wavetable Synthesis

If analogue synthesizers are made of electricity and instinct, wavetable synthesizers are built from possibility.

They bring a modern touch to the studio. Less concerned with recreating the past, they constantly ask what sound could become next. Their landscapes evolve, morph, and shimmer. They can imitate reality, but they truly come alive when they create something that has never existed before.

The Explorer

ASM Hydrasynth

Some synthesizers hide their power behind endless menus. The Hydrasynth does the opposite.

It may be one of the deepest instruments in my studio, yet somehow it remains one of the easiest to understand. Its workflow invites experimentation. One idea naturally leads to another. Complex modulation becomes intuitive, and happy accidents happen constantly.

It feels less like programming a machine and more like exploring an unknown world with an excellent map in your hands. Every time I sit in front of it, I discover a new path I had somehow missed before.

The Poet

Modal Argon8

The first thing you notice is the keyboard. It is simply magnificent. Responsive, expressive, and inviting — it makes you want to play long after the sound has faded away. And fortunately, the sound itself lives up to the touch.

The Argon8 has a unique elegance. Its wavetable engine produces tones that feel polished without losing warmth. It excels at pads that breathe, evolving textures, and melodies that seem suspended in mid-air.

If the Hydrasynth is the explorer, the Argon8 is the poet. It reminds you that technology can still be graceful.

The Alchemist

Waldorf Iridium Core

Some synthesizers specialise. The Iridium refuses.

Wavetable synthesis. Granular. Sampling. Virtual analogue. Resonators. It is less an instrument and more a laboratory for sound. The possibilities are almost overwhelming.

It can become nearly anything you imagine, but it asks something in return: patience, curiosity, and the willingness to lose yourself for hours while searching for a sound that has never existed before. This is not a synthesizer for shortcuts. It is a creation machine.

The Gentleman

Novation Peak

I love all my synthesizers. But the Peak occupies a special place in my heart.

It combines the richness and unpredictability that I adore in analogue instruments with the flexibility of modern digital design. Almost one knob per function — no fighting with menus, no interruption between inspiration and creation.

And then there is the reverb. It doesn't simply place sounds into a space. It sings. Pads bloom into cathedrals, leads float above the mix, and simple notes suddenly acquire emotion. Elegant without losing its soul.

If I had to choose one synthesizer outside of my beloved Moogs, this might be the one.

The Hidden Treasure

Roland Gaia 2

The Gaia 2 is often underestimated. At first glance, it seems simple. Approachable. Almost modest.

But spend time with it and another personality slowly emerges. Beneath its friendly surface lies an instrument capable of surprising complexity. Wavetables sit beside virtual analogue engines. Modulations create movement and life. Familiar sounds evolve into unexpected textures.

The Gaia never demands attention. Instead, it quietly whispers: "Have you tried this?" And before long, an hour has disappeared. Sometimes the most rewarding instruments are the ones that gently invite you to keep discovering.

05

The Korg Tribe

The Shape-Shifters

Some instruments find their voice and remain faithful to it for decades. The Korg tribe prefers transformation.

These synthesizers are restless creatures. They evolve, mutate, and challenge the idea that an instrument should have a single identity. They ask questions rather than provide answers: what if a sequence could become a landscape? What if FM synthesis could finally become musical instead of mathematical?

The Storyteller

Korg Wavestate

The Wavestate doesn't simply play notes. It tells stories.

Built around the idea of wave sequencing, it constantly rearranges itself. Samples evolve, rhythms shift, textures drift in and out of focus. A patch is never truly static. It breathes.

Press a key and what begins as a piano may become a choir, then dissolve into percussion before returning as something entirely unexpected. The Wavestate rewards patience and imagination. Sometimes cinematic. Sometimes nostalgic. Always moving.

The Surfer

Korg Modwave

The Modwave takes the world of wavetables and injects it with pure energy.

If the Wavestate tells stories, the Modwave rides waves. Its sounds twist and morph continuously, moving between aggression and beauty with astonishing ease. It excels at modern textures, evolving leads, and animated timbres that never seem to settle in one place.

Push it gently and it shimmers. Push it harder and it becomes wild. The Modwave reminds me that movement itself can be musical.

The Translator

Korg Opsix

For decades, FM synthesis had a reputation: powerful, complex, unforgiving. The Opsix changes that story.

It takes one of the most intimidating forms of synthesis ever created and translates it into something human. Suddenly, bells, electric pianos, metallic textures, crystalline pads, and impossible harmonics become invitations rather than puzzles.

There is still enormous depth beneath the surface, but the fear is gone. The Opsix proves that complexity does not have to be complicated. Sometimes all it takes is the right guide to reveal the beauty hidden behind mathematics.

Together, the Korg tribe reminds me that music is never fixed.

A melody can become a rhythm. A rhythm can become a texture. A texture can become an emotion. Everything can transform into something else.

And perhaps that is what creativity truly is: not inventing from nothing, but discovering what something else could become.

06

The Outlaws

The Ones Who Refused to Behave

Every orchestra has its virtuosos. Every family has its black sheep. These are mine.

The synthesizers that don't quite belong anywhere else. The rebels, the innovators, the strange little machines built by dreamers who decided that the established rules of synthesis were merely suggestions.

Sometimes they are brilliant. Sometimes they are infuriating. They are never boring.

The French Revolutionary

Norand Mono

This was my very first synthesizer. My gateway drug.

Born from French imagination, the Norand Mono answered an ambitious question: what if a modern synthesizer could offer deep modulation without screens, menus, or endless scrolling?

The answer was elegance. Every knob is surrounded by LEDs that quietly tell you where you stand. Everything remains immediate, tactile, alive.

At first glance, it almost resembles a Roland TB-303: compact, simple, approachable. Don't be fooled. Behind that minimalist interface hides a monster of modulation. It taught me that the deepest instruments don't always look complicated.

When Only Sound Matters

The Handmade Beast

At some point, practicality gave way to obsession.

Inside a Moog enclosure, I assembled an unlikely creature: a Dreadbox Hades living side by side with a Behringer Brain. No concern for appearances. No concern for conventions. Only sound.

And what a sound it is. Aggressive, metallic, unapologetic. Less synthesizer. More electric guitar forged from voltage. It doesn't ask for refinement. It demands attitude.

The Primitive Force

Behringer Edge

Do not try to control it.

The Edge has its own agenda. It grooves. It pulses. It spits out rhythms that feel older than melody itself. Primitive and hypnotic, it doesn't politely wait for instructions.

Instead, it presents an idea and dares you to build an entire track around it. Fight it, and you lose. Dance with it, and suddenly everything falls into place. This is techno in its purest form.

The Mad Inventors

Arturia MicroFreak & MiniFreak

If there is a glimpse of the future hidden in my studio, these two might be it.

Unlike almost anything else, they don't expose every parameter and don't pretend to satisfy the purists. Instead, they ask a simple question: what do you want to hear?

Granular textures. Virtual analogue warmth. Speech-like articulations. Metallic chaos. Delicate pads. They borrow the most inspiring ideas from the modular world and make them accessible without requiring a wall full of patch cables. Curious. Creative. Playful.

The Brothers at War

East Beast & West Pest

These two little semi-modular twins tell the entire story of synthesizer history.

The East Beast follows the East Coast philosophy: oscillators, filters, subtraction. Sculpting sound by removing frequencies until what remains feels right.

The West Pest walks another path. Wavefolders. Complex harmonics. Addition and transformation. Rather than carving away, it encourages sound to evolve into stranger and stranger forms.

Two philosophies. Two brothers. One eternal argument. But feeding the Moog Matriarch through the West Pest's wavefolder opens doors to textures neither instrument could discover alone. Sometimes the most beautiful sounds emerge not from choosing sides, but from letting opposites collaborate.

And perhaps that is the true lesson of this entire studio.

Analogue and digital. Precision and chaos. Tradition and innovation. East and West.

None of them are enemies. Music begins when they learn to speak to one another.

The Live Set

Reducing an Orchestra to Its Essence

There is, of course, one practical problem with building an orchestra of more than twenty synthesizers:

You can't fit it into the back of a car.

So every live performance begins with the same question:

"If I could only bring the essentials, what would they be?"

Packed inside a single flight case is my travelling studio: an Akai MPC One, a Dreadbox Erebus, the faithful Moog Minitaur, and two additional compact synthesizers chosen according to the mood of the set.

The MPC One becomes the conductor.

It sends MIDI sequences to the instruments travelling with me, allowing them to perform live while I shape the sounds in real time — opening filters, adding resonance, introducing movement, and responding to the energy of the audience.

The voices that stay behind in the studio aren't forgotten. Their parts are carefully recorded as audio tracks inside the MPC, preserving the textures and atmospheres that helped shape each piece.

The result sits somewhere between preparation and improvisation.

Some machines speak directly from the stage.

Others return as memories captured in sound.

But there is never a simple press of a play button.

Every performance evolves. Knobs are turned. Mistakes happen. Unexpected moments appear and disappear forever.

The tracks breathe differently each night.

Because behind all these machines, there is still a human being trying to transform electricity into emotion — and inviting a room full of strangers to share in the experience.
